\section*{Background}
\paragraph{Application.}
We teach a robot arm that manipulates one object to handle new ones from videos alone:
warehouse and home robots meet new items constantly, and recording paired images and
motor commands for each requires operating the robot. We study this in OCBench
simulation (UR5e arm, Robotiq gripper) \citep{ocbench2026}.

\paragraph{Problem and need.}
Visuomotor policies imitate images paired with motor commands, and acquiring such pairs
across varying configurations is costly and time-consuming: 50 people took a year to
collect 76k teleoperated demonstrations \citep{khazatsky2024droid}. With limited
training coverage, a policy may produce an erroneous action when it encounters an
unfamiliar situation. Errors can compound during execution, moving the robot further
from situations represented in its training data \citep{zhao2023act}. We investigate
whether action-free videos can help pixel-to-action policies manipulate new objects
without collecting additional action labels for those objects.

\section*{Aims/Goals}
\paragraph{Goals.}
Meet R4 and R5 on each target, satisfy R1--R3 by design, and measure R6.

\paragraph{Scope.}
We study an alternative data-gathering method in simulation, evaluating three target
objects and an optional fourth; real robots are unavailable. We use OCBench's visual
single-block task with its default $6 \times 6 \times 6$ cm red cube as source. Targets
are (A) a blue cube of identical size (appearance), (B) a $9 \times 6 \times 6$ cm red
block (geometry, same grasp width), (C) a $9 \times 7 \times 6$ cm red block (geometry
and grasp width), and optionally (D) a blue version of C (combined). Expert grasps are
adapted; robots, cameras, lighting, and tasks stay fixed. Human videos and large
pretrained models are out of scope.

\paragraph{Proposed solution.}
World models and video generation models could provide demonstrations without action
labels \citep{jang2025dreamgen}. In this project, we use simulator-generated videos of
new objects to evaluate learning from action-free demonstrations in a controlled
setting. We feed consecutive frames into an inverse dynamics model (IDM), trained on few
labeled cube demonstrations \citep{morin2026idmssil}, and infer actions for imitation
learning \citep{torabi2018bco}, \citep{baker2022vpt}. Formally, given cube transitions
$\mathcal{D}_A=\{(o_t,o_{t+1},a_t)\}$ and unlabeled target videos
$\mathcal{D}_B=\{o_t\}$, we seek an image-only policy $\pi(a \mid o)$ satisfying R1--R6.
We adapt the IDM to each target before labeling its videos, using frame differences for
motion cues and DANN \citep{ganin2016dann} to encourage domain-invariant features while
still predicting cube actions. One ACT policy per target then trains on cube
demonstrations plus pseudo-labeled target videos. Adaptation should help most on A,
where only appearance changes.

\section*{Methodology}
\paragraph{Design and implementation.}
Observations are $224 \times 224$ RGB images from the front, side, and wrist cameras;
actions are seven normalized joint and gripper increments at 50 Hz. The IDM uses a
convolutional encoder shared across cameras to embed consecutive frames and their
difference, followed by an MLP trained with action mean squared error. Following DANN
\citep{ganin2016dann}, a domain discriminator is trained with binary cross-entropy
through a gradient reversal layer, whose reversed gradient is scaled by a weight
$\lambda$ that ramps up during training. The IDM retains its supervised source
action-prediction loss; $\lambda = 0$ gives the unadapted IDM. The policy is an
image-only adaptation of ACT \citep{zhao2023act}, excluding joint-position inputs and
predicting chunks of 25 action increments. It is trained as a conditional VAE with L1
reconstruction and KL-divergence losses. Chunks are executed open loop without temporal
ensembling. IDM and policy encoder weights are separate. All policy comparisons use the
same ACT architecture and training budget in PyTorch.

\paragraph{Validation, testing, and evaluation.}
Demonstrations are split by episode into 80\% training, 10\% validation, and 10\% test,
with identical splits across methods. Target actions never select models: $\lambda$ is
prescheduled, IDM checkpoints follow source validation error, and policy training steps
are fixed. Across three seeds, we report per-component IDM error on held-out test
transitions, policy inference latency, and target success and cube retention over 100
separate evaluation rollouts per seed, method, and object, with identical initial states
across methods. We compare ACT policies using (a) cube-only data, (b) unadapted IDM
labels, (c) DANN-adapted IDM labels, (d) true target actions, and (e) cube-only
augmentation; optionally, (f) adds policy-encoder alignment to (b). Target actions
otherwise serve only post-training evaluation. R4 is inconclusive if (d)'s success does
not exceed (a)'s.

\paragraph{Alternatives.}
Methods (b)--(d) also use cube demonstrations; (b) and (c) share augmentation settings.
Comparing (b)/(c) isolates IDM adaptation; (e)/(c) tests gains over strong source-only
augmentation; (a)/(d) anchor R4. Method (e) uses hue, brightness, contrast, and
saturation jitter and small random crops. We expect it to be competitive on A; B and C
test geometry and grasp-width changes these augmentations do not directly reproduce. We
hypothesize that (c) improves pseudo-label accuracy and policy success over (b), while
testing for negative transfer.

\section*{Feasibility and resources}
\paragraph{Dataset, resources, and plan.}
We generate all data in OCBench with MuJoCo: 100 labeled cube demonstrations (300 if
cube-only ACT scores below 80\% in separate cube-validation rollouts; the budget and
split ratios are fixed before any comparison) and 200 action-free videos per target,
keeping true target actions only for evaluation and the oracle; no licensing or ethics
constraints apply. Training uses one NVIDIA A10G GPU; the pilot estimates compute. A
pilot by October 21 checks expert success, the cube-only policy's success on the cube
and each target, and the IDM's source validation error. Core methods follow by November
1; evaluation by November 18.

\section*{Risk analysis}
\paragraph{Pitfalls and mitigation.}
Adversarial alignment does not guarantee action alignment \citep{zhao2019invariant};
per-component errors test whether actions remain readable from arm motion. If experts
fail, adjust objects or grasps; if cube-only ACT fails on the cube, use OCBench's
shorter lite task. We report negative transfer if alignment hurts. If time runs short,
drop target D and policy-alignment experiment (f); oracle (d) remains mandatory for R4.
